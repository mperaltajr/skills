Build a client deck with Slide Lab, step by step

Accurate as of , Slide Lab version (the code's short ID). The newest copy is always in your Slide Lab folder: see Updates and help.

Slide Lab is a set of add-ons for Claude (the AI assistant you chat with in the Claude desktop app) that builds a client-branded PowerPoint deck with you. You bring the message, the audience, your notes and the client's PowerPoint template; Claude helps shape the story, designs every slide on that template, lets you pick the designs, checks the result and hands you a normal, editable .pptx file.

New to Slide Lab? Check Before you start, then follow the steps under Your first deck, one page each. In the middle of a deck? Click your step in the map below. Want to know how Claude shapes the argument, or what every slide must do? Read Storylines and Slide rules.

    Follow the install guide (a Word file; click to download it). Setup allows Slide Lab's own steps to run without asking each time; it does not allow anything else. The setup request:

Set up the client's template

Your turn Once per template

A template is the client's PowerPoint file with their colors, fonts and slide layouts. Registering it means telling Slide Lab which color does which job and which layouts to use. Claude works on its own copy; your original file is never changed. All of this happens in the chat.

Already set up? Go to step 3.

  1. Type Register my template at C:\path\to\client-template.pptx. Claude checks the slide size: it must be widescreen (16:9) at 13.333 x 7.5 inches, PowerPoint's standard widescreen size.
    You'll seeA message in the chat about the slide size.
  2. Answer three color questions, one at a time: the main brand color, the highlight color and the cover background. In the same pass Claude asks whether you have a real deck that uses this template. Give its path, or say no. With a deck, Claude picks 3 or 4 of its best content pages so the designs match the client's look.
    You'll seeA list of every color Claude found, each with a plain name, its color code and where it appears ("dark navy, 0B3C49, title bars on content slides"). Claude never pre-picks an answer: the automatic guess was wrong on every template tried so far.
  3. Pick the default content layout (used for most slides) and the cover layout from the pictures Claude shows. Optional: name one slide in the template that shows how every content slide should look.
    You'll seeA picture of each layout in the chat. Claude will not finish without the default content layout, because later builds need it.
  4. Claude builds one sample slide on the template and gives you its path. Open it in PowerPoint. Check the title, takeaway, footnote and source sit where they should and fit, and that the main and highlight colors are not swapped. Tell Claude "it looks right".
    You'll seeClaude marks the template confirmed and adds it to your pick-list.
TipUnsure which color is which? Open a real deck from the client: the main color carries titles and bars, the highlight is used sparingly for emphasis.

Your first deck, step 3 of 8

3

Shape the storyline with Claude

Your turn A conversation in the chat

Before any slide exists, Claude coaches the argument. The result is a dot-dash storyline: one bold claim per slide (the dot) with its supporting points (the dashes). Read the dots top to bottom and they should tell the whole story. How Claude coaches it, and what it checks: How Slide Lab builds a storyline.

Claude writes each headline as a fact the audience can check, with a number where there is one ("Only Vietnam and Indonesia clear both the size and readiness bar"), not a slogan ("Vietnam is the clear winner"). Claude also saves a brief, the detailed plan the slide designs are built from.

  1. Describe the deck: number of slides, main message, audience, and paste your notes or numbers. For example: Build me a 10-slide deck. Main message: ... Audience: ... Here are my notes: ...
    You'll seeClaude says back what it saw in your material and asks 1 to 3 pointed questions.
  2. Answer, push back, reword. If a slide's claim is not what you mean, say so in your words; it rewrites.
    You'll seeClaude proposes one structure for the deck with its reasons, then goes slide by slide: the claim first, then the support.
  3. Read the summary message Claude sends, then reply once: type Build to accept the rewrites and build the slides, "save only" to stop with the storyline, or say what to change. To keep a flagged line as it is, say why in your own words; "skip" alone is not accepted. Until you answer, nothing is saved or built.
    You'll seeThe summary message, in three parts:
    1. The setup: the session folder (it suggests C:\Users\<you>\Slide Lab\sessions\<Client>\), the client and topic, the template and the default content layout.
    2. A table of any problems in the storyline, each with a suggested rewrite: a headline that is a slogan instead of a fact, a claim with no number behind it, or a headline that will not fit on one line in the template's own font (what each problem means).
    3. The question below.

    Claude asks"Build, save only, or tell me what to change?"

    You'll seeAfter your answer, file paths in the chat: Claude saves the storyline (as a Word file, a text file and a web page), the brief and a list of open gaps in the session folder's _session folder. With "build", it goes straight on to step 4.
Note"Save only" is a normal finish if you only needed the storyline: Stop here, I only need the storyline

Storylines

How Slide Lab builds a storyline

Before any slide exists, Claude works through the argument with you in the chat (step 3). A deck with a strong argument and rough slides lands; a deck with polished slides and no argument fails. So Claude sharpens your thinking, but it does not write the argument for you: it reads what you give it, asks for what is missing and pushes back where a point is unclear. It never invents an argument or a number you did not give it.

Time
Your replies
Usually 7 to 12 for a 9-slide deck
You get
The storyline (a Word file, a text file and a web page), the brief the designs are built from, and a list of open gaps

The five stages

With Build, Claude saves the files and goes straight on to the designs (step 4). With save only, it stops; building later needs nothing redone.

How Claude works with you

  • It works out the deck type itself. It never asks you to choose from a list. You see the type at the top of the problems table and in the saved storyline.
  • It picks the structure; it does not hand you a menu. It names the runner-up it rejected and why. Push back if you disagree. It may also suggest a strategy framework to think the content through (for example Porter's five forces, Blue Ocean, or Rumelt's diagnosis, policy and action); those are offered, never imposed.
  • Its questions are about substance. At most 3 in its first reply. Setup questions (folder, template) wait for the summary message at the end.
  • It asks for a missing fact at most twice. If you have none, the point stays as a judgment, marked "qualitative", and goes on the open-gaps list. It never makes up a number.
  • It pushes back in four beats: names the problem, says what it costs, shows a better version, and asks one question.
  • You can keep most of what it flags, with your reason. Say why in your own words; "skip" is not a reason. Claude writes your reason into the brief's Flags list, with what the audience might see. Some things cannot be kept: anything Critical, two slides that contradict each other, a slide with no "what this slide is not" line, and a content slide too thin to stand on its own.
  • If you have almost nothing, it asks three questions first: the one sentence the reader should take away, what they believe now that you want to change, and the hardest objection they would raise. It does not order slides until those have answers.
  • If you already wrote the storyline, it does not start over. It asks whether you want a review (a table of problems and suggested fixes, nothing built) or a build. A web-page mockup is read for its content and order; the slides are designed fresh on the client's template, so the mockup's exact look does not carry over, and Claude says so before building.
  • If you are fluent, it coaches less. It names a structure without explaining it and keeps pushback short, but the check at the end is just as strict.

Page order and page types

Page order

NoteSlide Lab has no fixed page order for each deck type. Claude builds the order from your material, using the structure it picked, and then checks it. These are the only parts of the order Slide Lab fixes.
  • Recommendation and business case decks need a page that names the decision and a date, and a place for the obvious objections: a risk page, or the alternatives considered and rejected.
  • Operating reviews may end "for information"; they do not need a decision page.
  • Every deck can state its answer in 30 seconds, has no page without a point, and does not bury the recommendation at the end.
  • Workshop design is the one fixed order: purpose and objectives; agenda; session objectives (optional); pre-work; ground rules (optional).
  • A cover page carries the title, a tagline, a subtitle, the presenter, the date and the audience. A closing page that asks for something carries one main ask and three supporting asks.

For a full order Claude built, see Worked example: Meridian.

One slide in the storyline

Each slide in the storyline is a dot (the slide's claim, which becomes its headline) and dashes (the supporting points). Under the headline sits the takeaway, one line that says what the facts mean. Meridian's slide 4:

Dot (the headline)

Takeaway (the line under it)

What the storyline check flags

At the end of the conversation Claude checks the whole storyline before anything is saved or designed. What it finds is the problems table in the summary message (step 3), every row with a proposed rewrite. The check has two halves: Claude's review of the argument, and a measured check that a script runs against your template.

What happens to a flagged line

  • Critical Must be fixed; it cannot be kept. Two slides that contradict each other must always be reconciled.
  • Major Fix it, or keep it with your reason in your own words ("skip" is not a reason). Your reason goes into the brief's Flags list, with what the audience might see.
  • Advisory A judgment call; it never blocks.

Nothing is saved until you answer, even when the table holds only Advisory rows. Once you answer, the storyline is sealed; if the brief is edited later, the check runs again before anything is built.

Slide rules

Slide rules: how Slide Lab checks your slides

Every slide Slide Lab builds follows one set of rules. The designers are given them before they draw, and the review pages and the quality check catch what slips through. This part lists only rules Slide Lab actually applies: what each one asks, why, and where it is checked.

The short version

  1. One claim per slide: the headline is a full sentence with a specific fact, on one line (more)
  2. The line under the title says what the facts mean, with one of the page's numbers (more)
  3. No buzzwords: write the number or fact they stand for (more)
  4. Body text pt, at most sizes on a slide, never under pt (more)
  5. Every number and label comes from your brief; nothing is invented (more)
  6. Real charts only, with units and honest axes (more)
  7. One highlighted element per slide (more)
  8. No three slides in a row on the same layout (more)
  9. Number only real sequences, and give every item a simple marker (more)
  10. Every outside number names its source, and no source line is made up (more)

The five rules every design follows

  1. Real charts and tables only. No chart-like pictures made of boxes in a card layout; a small sparkline inside another layout is fine.
  2. Nothing beyond what the brief lists. If the brief names 2 options, the slide shows 2.
  3. No three slides in a row on the same layout. Two in a row is fine.
  4. Every visible word comes from the brief or the template's own furniture (footer, page number, section label).
  5. If the brief and the layout disagree, the designer refuses that layout and the review page says so, instead of forcing the content to fit.

Help and common questions

Common questions

Do I need to know how to code or design?

No. You talk to Claude in plain English and click buttons on two web pages. Claude asks before it runs anything on your computer; click Allow when it does.

Why did Claude stop and wait?

Slide Lab is built to stop at certain points and wait for your answer. A stop is not a bug: Claude is asking for something only you can decide. Nothing moves on until you answer.

If you do not answer: nothing is built, saved or deleted. Answer in the chat, or click the button on the page it opened.

What if I don't like any of the designs for a slide?

Click REPLACE THESE and say in the notes what you want different. You get new design by default; say "with 3 options" if you want alternatives. For a small change, pick the closest option and add a quick-feedback button or a note instead.

Can I get every option in one PowerPoint file?

Yes. All options in one deck on the review page builds every option, not just your picks, into one deck for side-by-side comparison. It costs several times more than a normal build, so the page warns you and asks before it copies anything. Use it rarely.

Why do I paste a message back into Claude?

The review pages are files on your computer and cannot talk to Claude directly. The copied message carries your exact choices with a check code, so nothing is retyped or misread. Paste it unchanged.

Can I keep my work in OneDrive?

Keep the session folder on your computer's own drive (C:\Users\<you>\Slide Lab\sessions\). In OneDrive, builds are noticeably slower and previews can time out. Copy the finished deck to OneDrive or SharePoint afterward. The template itself can live anywhere.

Can I edit the charts afterward?

Charts are drawn from shapes for a clean, on-brand look, so they are not linked to a datasheet. Either ask Claude to rebuild the slide with new numbers, or say "Export the chart data to Excel" and paste the sheet into think-cell or a normal PowerPoint chart. The chart rules: Numbers and charts.

Is my client's template safe?

Yes. Slide Lab works on its own copy and never changes your original file. Your work stays on your computer.

Can it work on a deck I already have?

Yes, "Change a deck" in the menu: fix text or numbers, redesign a slide and drop it back into your deck, or refresh a recurring deck with this cycle's text. It always writes a new file. See Change a deck later.

Does it work on a Mac?

Yes. The install guide covers Windows and Mac. Paths look different on a Mac (your home folder instead of C:\Users\<you>).

Why did Claude rewrite my headline?

A headline must be a claim with a specific number or name, and it must fit one line of the client's title box, measured in the template's own font. Claude shows every rewrite in one table in the summary message. To keep your wording, say why in your own words. See Headlines, supporting points and takeaways.

Can I break a slide rule?

Often, with a reason. A Major finding can stay if you say why in your own words; Claude records your reason. A Critical finding must be fixed, and two slides that contradict each other must be reconciled. See Slide rules.
